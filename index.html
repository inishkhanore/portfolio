Putting an 8.5 GHz signal generator to work in an NV-centre magnetometer

Summer intern with Dr. Phani Peddibhotla, Department of Physics, IISER Bhopal

The physics

A nitrogen-vacancy centre in diamond fluoresces a little less when a microwave field hits its spin resonance near 2.87 GHz. A magnetic field along the NV axis splits that resonance in two, and the gap between the dips gives the field. Optically detected magnetic resonance (ODMR) is the sweep that finds them.

f± = D ± γB, with D = 2.870 GHz and γ = 28.0 GHz/T. Each pass adds noise the way a real sweep does, and averaging pulls the dips out.

The build
  • Integrated the Zurich Instruments SHFSG+ into the lab’s ODMR workflow, with LabVIEW and LabOne automation and hardware-triggered control.
  • Wrote hardware-timed RF pulse sequences in SeqC: frequency sweeps, deterministic timing, oscillator phase control, and microwave output synchronised with data acquisition.
  • Worked through the RF chain: IQ and single-sideband modulation, synthesizers and upconversion, DAC amplitude, output-power calibration and 50 Ω signal integrity.
  • Routed hardware triggers between the SHFSG+ and an NI USB-6363 DAQ, and tracked down timing and execution-queue synchronisation faults in the control workflow.
  • Ran continuous-wave, stepped and hardware-triggered ODMR to validate the framework across acquisition modes.
  • Wrote the SHFSG+ user manual for the lab, covering operation, LabVIEW and LabOne integration, and troubleshooting, so new members can get started.

Mapping the field of a bar magnet

The physics

The field of a permanent magnet is a magnetostatics problem, so it can be solved numerically. I modelled the magnet in COMSOL, then checked the model against the real one with a Hall probe.

Abstract accepted Khanore, I. (2026). COMSOL Magnetostatic Simulation of a Permanent Bar Magnet with Gaussmeter Validation. COMSOL Conference 2026 Bengaluru, India, December 2026.
The build

I automated the gauss probe in LabVIEW and mapped the field point by point. Each of the 90 readings is a position, an angle and a field strength.

Each tick lies along the field at that point, and its colour is the field strength on a log scale. Hover or tap one to read it.

A vibrating-sample magnetometer, as a proof of concept

The physics

A vibrating-sample magnetometer measures a sample’s magnetic moment by shaking it in a uniform field. The moving moment induces a voltage in a nearby pickup coil at the vibration frequency, and a lock-in amplifier pulls that signal out of the noise.

What the lock-in saw36 readings from the bench

The signal at the vibration frequency rises with drive frequency. The spikes are stray pickup. Hover or tap for values.

The build
  • A Helmholtz-coil electromagnet for the uniform field, matched against a Lakeshore 410 gaussmeter.
  • A 4 Ω, 5 W speaker to vibrate the sample, driven from a signal generator.
  • A 200-turn air-core pickup coil wound from 27-gauge enamelled copper wire.
  • A sample cut from an old hard-disk platter and magnetised in an external field.
  • An SR830 lock-in amplifier, with a LabVIEW program that steps the drive frequency and logs the amplitude.

The lock-in detected a voltage induced at the sample’s vibration frequency, with some noise at grid frequencies. There was more to refine than the internship had time for.

February to March 2026

Hydrogen orbital visualizer

The physics

The quantum numbers n, l and m set an orbital’s energy and shape. Solving the Schrödinger equation for hydrogen gives the wavefunction ψ as a radial part times a spherical harmonic.

Pick one to load it into the cloud at the top of the page.

The build

A Python script, using NumPy, SciPy and Matplotlib, draws 50,000 coordinates by Monte Carlo sampling weighted by |ψ|² and renders them as a 3D point cloud. The inferno colormap makes the likeliest regions glow brightest.

It began as a Python port inspired by kavan010/Atoms. The cloud at the top of this page uses the same method, rewritten to run in the browser.

January 2026

Two-axis optical mount for aligning a lenslet array

The physics

A Shack–Hartmann wavefront sensor only works when its lenslet array is in focus and aligned in angle with the camera behind it. That needs two fine, repeatable adjustments that stay put once set.

The build

I designed and 3D printed a compact optomechanical mount with two independent degrees of freedom: linear translation for focus and rotation for angular alignment. It fits C-mount (1.00-32 TPI) optics, locks after alignment, and suits CCD camera assemblies in optical and photonic setups.
